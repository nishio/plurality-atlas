What is Reverse Alignment?

The other side of "alignment", which fits AI to humans: an effort to rebuild society's institutions, rules, people's skills and systems of governance so that AI can be used safely and its benefits widely shared. Glen Weyl and others put it forward as an agenda in 2026.

When people talk about AI safety, the usual question is how to fit AI to human values. This research is called "alignment". But even a perfectly well-behaved AI could disappoint, or do harm, if it arrives in a society that isn't ready to receive it. Reverse alignment is an idea that focuses on this readiness on society's side.

In short

Reverse alignment means rebuilding society's institutions, rules, people's skills and systems of governance so that AI can be used safely and its benefits widely shared [1][2]. It is an agenda put forward in 2026 by the economist E. Glen Weyl and others.

Weyl's slides present the two as a pair: alignment brings the AI side in line with human values, while reverse alignment prepares the institutional side to receive it [3].

Note that another paper uses the same term in an entirely different sense. This is explained below under "What is debated".

For example

An essay in Noema magazine (July 21, 2026). Weyl, James Evans and Chris White published it together with a coalition of 23 people from different fields, regions and political positions. The essay opens with an event: the US government ordered the AI company Anthropic to stop offering its top model to foreign nationals, and within hours the company stopped it for all customers. The essay points out that the institutions for governing, absorbing and understanding AI have not kept pace with the growth of its capabilities [1].

Historical precedents. The same essay lists cases where the benefits of technology spread thanks to institutional innovation: the institutions of mass democracy that emerged after 19th-century industrialization (antitrust laws, independent regulators, labor unions and so on), the postwar international financial framework, ARPANET (the forerunner of the internet), India's digital infrastructure India Stack, and Taiwan's digital democracy [1]. Weyl's slides sum this up as "the lesson of the dynamo": technology waits decades for institutions to catch up [3].

The public lecture "Platform Originals" (August 29, 2026). Audrey Tang noted that when factories and mines first appeared in Britain, it "took one or two generations" before limits on child labor were written into institutions. With AI, she said, what we do not want is "the British factories again, where you wait one or two generations, a whole childhood spent inside the problem," before compensation comes [4][5].

How it works

Three failures if nothing is done

The Noema essay names three failures that occur when society doesn't prepare [1][2].

  1. Productivity without prosperity — Jobs are reorganized faster than people can retrain or move. The gains that are created then fail to reach most people.
  2. Execution without verification — The cost of producing text, images and code falls almost to zero. What becomes scarce is the human capacity to check whether what was produced is genuine, trace where it came from, and take responsibility for it.
  3. Capacity without constraint — The same tools also lower the cost for states and large organizations to watch people. Unless the mechanisms for objecting and for protecting freedom grow alongside, power concentrates in one place.

The essay argues that none of these is inevitable fate; each is the result of neglecting institutions [1].

Lopsided spending

The essay also takes issue with the fact that, compared with the money spent on making AI smarter, only a tiny share goes to the institutions, education, organizations and civic infrastructure needed to integrate AI into society [1].

Twelve challenges

The coalition's website divides the work into 12 areas [2]:

  • Identity (showing you are a real, unique human without revealing who you are)
  • Privacy, proof of provenance, and data value (payment for the human record used to train AI)
  • Collaboration between AIs, collective sensemaking, democracy, and law and freedom
  • The workplace, research, education, and transitions in work

Many of these overlap with topics the book Plurality covers: proof of personhood, data dignity, bridging and broad listening.

The hypothesis that institutions resemble their tools

The coalition has a distinctive hypothesis: when a society absorbs a new technology, the institutions that succeed come to resemble the shape of that era's central technology [2].

In the industrial era, for example, standardized accounting and the assembly line gave rise to hierarchical corporations and central banks. In the internet era, layered communication protocols gave rise to organizations like layered, cooperative standards bodies. Neural networks (the basis of today's AI) find shared structure without averaging differences away, and trace contributions through to results. The coalition expects that institutions able to receive AI will imitate this behavior at the scale of society [2].

Who is behind it

The coalition's website lists both Weyl and Tang among its members [2]. There are also Japanese and Chinese-language versions of the site [6][7]. In September 2026, the organization RadicalxChange announced that it would take on the democracy area among the 12 challenges [8].

What is debated

  • Beware a different concept with the same name. In an April 2026 paper, David Jacob Harrison discussed "the reverse alignment problem". This refers to a phenomenon in which, instead of machines being fitted to humans, human values and goals become simpler and people are made more machine-like. It points in the opposite direction from Weyl and colleagues' proposal to deliberately rebuild society's institutions [9]. This article uses the term in Weyl and colleagues' sense.
  • How effective altruists (EA) received it. On the EA Forum, Tom Vargas summarized the core point this way: if institutions are wrong, adding advanced AI that is well aligned with humans will probably still make outcomes worse. Vargas found it plausible, and wrote that because it deals with the foundations of society it is very hard to carry out, but could change things far more if it works. In the comments, some said in effect that aligning individual models does not guarantee that the whole system using them is aligned [10].
  • "Who aligns to whom?" At the lecture, someone asked whether reverse alignment is about deciding who has to accommodate whom. Tang answered that past institutional responses to industrialization, electrification and the internet all answered the same questions: "If you are not satisfied, whom do you appeal to? How do you come to know? How do you opt out, or switch to another system?" For AI, she said, the hope is that it will look more like the internet: small-scale trials, and the ability to build better ways yourselves [4].

Where it fits in Plurality

The term "reverse alignment" does not appear in the book Plurality (English edition). It is a claim made in 2026, after the book, by Weyl and the coalition. Care is needed not to mix it up with what the book says.

Still, its substance connects closely with the book. In its conclusion, the Noema essay's prescription is cooperation that bridges divided communities, places trust not in central authorities but in diverse webs of human relationships, and finds common ground without suppressing disagreement [1]. That is the idea of collaboration across social difference at the heart of the book. Its use of Taiwan as an example also draws on the same material as Part 2 of the book (Taiwan's digital democracy).

The 6-Pack of Care and Civic AI, in which Tang is involved, sit close by. Both agree that fixing what is inside the AI is not enough on its own. The 6-Pack is a framework for designing procedures in the places where AI works, while reverse alignment is an agenda calling for investment in society's institutions as a whole [2][11]. For how this relates to debates about the future of AI, see the AI futures debate.
