What is open source?

A way of making software in which its blueprint (the source code) is published so that anyone can use, study, fix and redistribute it. The book Plurality sees it as a major example in practice of ⿻ (Plurality: diverse people collaborating across their differences), and names as a problem the fact that little money reaches the people who do this work.

Software has a blueprint that people can read and rewrite, called source code. There is a way of making software in which this blueprint is published, so that anyone in the world can use it, fix it and redistribute it. That is open source. The book Plurality sees in it a model for a society in which diverse people collaborate.

In short

Open source is a way of making software in which the source code is published so that anyone can use, study, fix and redistribute it. People who share an interest build on each other's work, and when they cannot agree, they "fork" the project — branch off — and make a separate version [1].

Plurality sees this as a large-scale practice of ⿻ (Plurality: diverse people collaborating across their differences) [1]. At the same time, it names as a policy issue the fact that almost no money reaches the people who do this work [2].

For example

This book itself. The author profile in Plurality says: "This book is open-source and its contents may be freely copied, with or without attribution." [3] According to chapter 5-0, the text was stored and updated using Git, the version-control tool that open-source developers use, and released under CC0 (a declaration that gives up copyright so that anyone may use the work freely). Contributions were recorded using GitRules (formerly Gov4Git, an open-source tool that runs on top of Git and records who contributed how much and which changes are accepted), on a distributed ledger (a record that the participants all keep copies of) [4].

The same chapter asks readers who are inspired by the content, "especially critically", to submit changes or to reach out to one of the contributors and become part of the community. "We hope as many criticisms of this work as possible will be inspired by the open-source mantra 'so fix it!'" [4]

vTaiwan and Join (Taiwan). Taiwan has vTaiwan, a forum for discussion started by citizens, and Join, a government-run site for proposals and public comment [5]. In a 2018 conversation, Audrey Tang (唐鳳), who served as Taiwan's first Minister of Digital Affairs [3], was asked why vTaiwan is open source while Join is not. Tang answered that Join was a project begun at the end of 2014, for which a contractor was chosen by public tender in 2015, and that at the time, the template the Taiwanese government used when buying information systems did not yet contain a section on open source [6].

Gitcoin Grants (funding). Chapter 5-7 of the book says that because software is made freely available, open source "has long struggled for reliable sources of funding" [7]. It then describes new "matching platforms" such as Gitcoin Grants, which distribute the money that sponsors (small donors and grant-makers) put in, using a formula that "accounts not just for the total funding received, but also the diversity of its source across individual contributors and connected social groups". These platforms "have become important sources of funding for OSS [open-source software], channeling in total more than a hundred million dollars in funding" [7].

In more detail

Where it came from

According to chapter 3-3 of the book, open source "emerged in reaction to the secretive and commercial direction of the software industry that emerged in the 1970s" [1]. The book says that the free and open way of developing software in the early days of ARPANET, the forerunner of the internet, was kept alive by volunteers around the world even after public funding was withdrawn [1].

Richard Stallman led the free software movement from the 1980s onward, a movement that promoted users' entitlement to run, study, share and modify source code [1]. In 1998, the Open Source Initiative gave the practical aspects of this the name "open source" [1].

Three names

Tang distinguishes between different names for the same thing. In the 2018 conversation, Tang said that to say "free software" is to make an argument about human rights and morality: the right to use and modify software must not be taken away. To say "open source" is to make an argument about efficiency and economics: the more people take part in development, and the more transparently, the better the quality [6]. Tang added, however, that the substance of the two kinds of license (the terms on which the software may be used) is almost the same [6].

On the podcast Tech Mirror in 2024, Tang explained that in Taiwan it is called public code, meaning that code is treated as public infrastructure, like airports and train stations [8]. In a 2023 speech, Tang mentioned "Public Money? Public Code!", a campaign holding that systems the government builds with taxpayers' money should be open, and said that Taiwan had persuaded the cabinet that code, like bridges and highways, is eligible for the public infrastructure budget [9].

A model of ⿻

Chapter 3-3 says that the spread of open source, "even earning support from formerly hostile companies like Microsoft", "represents the practice of ⿻ on a large scale". Its example, Microsoft, owns GitHub, the major service where open-source developers gather, and employs one of the book's authors [1].

What the book focuses on is the shape of the collaboration: "Communities form around shared interests, freely build on each other's work, vet contributions through unpaid maintainers, and 'fork' projects into parallel versions in case of irreconcilable differences" [1]. (Maintainers are the people who look after a project and check others' contributions.) Chapter 4-3 treats an open-source project as an example in which collaboration often creates more value than individuals acting alone, and in which the result is used by many people together. In such situations, it argues, "money-based management does not function well" [10].

Money does not reach it

Chapter 3-3 cites Working in Public: The Making and Maintenance of Open Source Software (2020), a book by Nadia Eghbal about how open-source software is made and kept going, and lists the challenges: a chronic shortage of financial support due to the withdrawal of public funding, maintainers who often go unrewarded, and a burden that grows as the community grows. Even so, the book says, "these challenges are addressable" [1].

Chapter 2-0 says that open source software and other commons-based public goods like Wikipedia (public goods, in economists' terms, are things like streetlights and lighthouses: one person's use does not reduce what is left for others, and people who do not pay cannot be shut out [11]) "have become critical public resources in the digital age", yet governments have failed to support them and have even treated them worse than other charities: "open source software providers generally cannot be tax-exempt charities" [12].

Chapter 7-0 says that the part of the digital ecosystem most neglected by governments today is not academia but "the largely ignored world of open-source and other non-profit, mission-driven technology developers" [2]. Academic research receives hundreds of billions of dollars a year, while, in the English edition, open-source communities "have likely received less than two billion dollars in their entire history, accounting for known sources", as shown in a chart that compiles sources such as GitHub Sponsors, Gitcoin and Open Collective [2]. The Japanese edition gives a different figure here: probably less than one billion dollars (おそらく10億ドル未満) [13].

Talking about open source rather than public goods

In a 2025 post, Vitalik Buterin, the founder of Ethereum, argued that funding is better discussed in terms of "open source" than of "public goods" [14]. The reasons given: the term "public good" is also used to mean something produced by a government, and its meaning has become so diluted that even commercial projects with their own tokens (a kind of crypto asset) present themselves as building public goods. "Open source", by contrast, has a clear definition that has stood for decades [14].

In the same year, Buterin wrote another post explaining a shift: Buterin used to prefer permissive licenses, among other reasons in order to have the work adopted as widely as possible, but now leans toward copyleft (licenses that require modified versions to be shared on the same terms) [15].

Digital public goods

A column (written by tokoroten, i.e. Shinta Nakayama) in 政治・自治体・企業に広がるブロードリスニング (Broad listening spreading through politics, local government and business), a Japanese book about efforts to hear citizens' voices widely, notes that in 2020 the United Nations defined open-source software, data, AI models, standards and content as "digital public goods". The definition comes with conditions: they must comply with privacy and other applicable laws, do no harm, and help achieve the UN's Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [11].

The same column notes that a 2025 report by Japan's Information-technology Promotion Agency (IPA) pointed out that Japanese government bodies publish far less open source than those of the US, the UK and France. Arguing that the people who can put it to good use are the real cost, the column says that "digital public goods are free, but not zero-cost" (translated from the Japanese) [11].

What is debated

Should AI be open too? Chapter 7-0 of the book says that some organizations developing generative foundation models, "such as OpenAI and Anthropic, have legitimate concerns about simply making these models freely available" [2].

On the podcast Your Undivided Attention in 2024, the host Aza Raskin introduced what he called "one of the principles I think you stand for, Audrey": "democracy should have the ability to retune these powerful AI models to be in service of strengthening democracy, and for that, it really helps for them to be open source and open weight" (open weight: the trained model is published so that anyone can retune it). Raskin then added that the spread of open models harms democracies more, because their information systems are open, and asked how to tell when to release such models and when to hold them back [16]. Tang answered with a pandemic analogy: "the more a technology looks like surgical-grade masks, the more we should make it open source." The closer a technology is to something with a very high lethality rate, the more regulations, drills and audits should be required before it is released; or maybe it should never be released [16].

In a 2017 paper, the philosopher Nick Bostrom wrote that the short-term impacts of more openness in AI development "appear mostly socially beneficial in expectation" [17]. On the other hand, the paper argues that openness about source code, science "and possibly capability" could make the race to develop advanced AI closer around the time it arrives, "increasing the probability that winning the AI race is incompatible with using any safety method that incurs a delay or limits performance". Openness about safety measures and about goals, it says, is "plausibly positive" both for the present generation and for the well-being of future generations [17].

Is it insecure, or a way to save money? On Tech Mirror in 2024, the host said that Taiwan's experience of withstanding daily cyberattacks from China disproves the myth that open source cannot be secure. Tang replied that public code is not about saving costs; at first it actually costs more, because a community of practitioners who know how to use it has to be built [8]. The column's point above, that digital public goods are free but not zero-cost, points in the same direction [11].

Practical, not a dream. In a 2019 interview, Tang was asked whether Silicon Valley's dream of an open-source and free internet was still alive, and answered: "Why would you consider that a dream? It's a very practical thing." As examples, Tang said that Microsoft "did a U-turn" and now owns GitHub, and that Google would not exist without Linux, the open-source operating system (the basic software that runs a computer) [18].

Where it fits in Plurality

Chapter 3-3 of Plurality, "The Lost Dao", treats open source as a model of how the open, collaborative spirit of the people who built the internet still continues. For this reason, it says, "OSS projects will be frequent examples in this book" [1].

Chapter 5-7, "Social Markets", opens with the problem of funding open source and with Gitcoin Grants [7]. Chapter 7-0, "Policy", imagines that if ⿻ succeeds, in a decade public funding will be the main source of financial support for fundamental digital protocols [2]. The same chapter also says that "open-source communities are just the tip of the iceberg in terms of what may be possible for public-interested, civil society-driven technology development", since foundations such as Mozilla and Wikimedia have significant activities beyond writing code [2].

Other chapters have examples too. Chapter 6-4 names LASS, a citizen-run network of environmental sensors in Taiwan, and chapter 6-5 names Moedict (萌典), a dictionary of Mandarin, Taiwanese and Hakka, as examples of open-source collaboration [19][20].
