What is Digital Democracy 2030 (DD2030)?

A volunteer community in Japan that builds tools to help citizens' voices reach politics and government, as open source that anyone can use for free. Takahiro Anno and Ken Suzuki started it in January 2025, and it develops Kouchou AI, Idobata and Polimoney. It commits itself to neutrality, favoring no party.

When you write an opinion in a city hall survey, it is hard to see how it was read or used. Reading thousands of free-form answers one by one is also a heavy load for staff. Digital Democracy 2030 (DD2030) is a group trying to build tools for "getting voices heard and hearing voices" together, and hand them out.

In short

DD2030 is a volunteer community in Japan with the goal that, by 2030, it will be normal in society for each person's voice to reach politics and government [1][2]. It builds tools for this for free, as open source (publishing the blueprint of a program so anyone can reuse it).

It commits itself to "political neutrality", favoring no particular party, and provides its tools on the same terms to local governments and to both governing and opposition parties [1].

For example

Kouchou AI. A tool that uses AI to organize free-form answers in local government surveys. The town of Utazu in Kagawa Prefecture, Iwate Prefecture, Shibuya Ward, Hiroshima Prefecture and others have tried it [3][4]. It is covered in the article on Kouchou AI.

Idobata. A tool that gathers opinions as each person chats one-on-one with an AI, and pulls them together into issues and proposals for improving policy. It supports deliberation (discussing things carefully). It has been used in drawing up Team Mirai's manifesto and inside the company Cybozu [5]. See the article on Idobata.

Parties taking part. According to the news site Japan In-depth, right after the launch, Kazuya Shinba, secretary-general of the Democratic Party for the People, spoke of having been deeply moved watching the press conference, and had Takae Ito, the party's member of the House of Councillors, take part [6].

Making a book. Broad Listening (Japanese title 政治・自治体・企業に広がるブロードリスニング, Impress, due September 2026) was written by volunteers as part of DD2030's activities. There are also web versions in Japanese and English [7][2].

In more detail

The launch

Takahiro Anno announced DD2030 at a press conference on January 16, 2025 [2][8]. The book Broad Listening introduces it as "a nonprofit open-source project jointly launched in January 2025 by AI engineer Takahiro Anno and Ken Suzuki, Chairman of the Board at SmartNews and Project Researcher at the University of Tokyo" [7].

Its foundation was "Team Anno", the volunteers who supported Anno in the 2024 governor's election. It was created so that the experience of broad listening (listening widely to many voices) built up in the governor's election, in Nippon TV's special program on the House of Representatives election and in Tokyo's long-term strategy would not end as one-off projects. The same book writes that the aim was to deliver it continuously to society "in a form that municipalities, political parties, and politicians could use neutrally" [7].

At the press conference, Anno called 2025 "year one of digital democracy" [2].

What it works on

At the launch, three projects were announced [2][9].

  1. Taking broad listening further — improving a tool called Talk to the City. This later became Kouchou AI (released March 16, 2025) [7].
  2. Reflecting public opinion in policy — a place for large-scale online discussion, modeled on Taiwan's JOIN and vTaiwan. This later became Idobata.
  3. Making political funds transparent — Polimoney. In Sweden, cabinet ministers are given official credit cards and the statements are made public. Taking this as a reference, Polimoney shows the flow of political funds with graphs and other visuals [10].

At the time the materials for this article were collected (September 2026), the official website listed five projects, adding two to these. One is spreading the ideas of broad listening widely (writing the book). The other is "Project Coreloop". Coreloop aims not to stop at listening to voices, but to connect them all the way through building agreement, policy and carrying out budgets, to people actually feeling the improvement [1].

Neutrality and governance

The official page explains "political neutrality" in two senses: "partisan neutrality", not siding with any particular party or candidate, and "procedural neutrality", dealing with everyone on the same terms and through the same procedures. It then adds that this does not mean staying out of politics altogether. It aims to cover its operating costs with donations from the general public [11].

At the same point, the board members (who run the organization) were Ken Suzuki, Haruyuki Seki (Code for Japan) and Makiko Nakamuro (Keio University). Anno resigned in May 2025 to maintain political neutrality, and Oshiba (大柴行人) resigned in January 2026 [11]. According to the book Broad Listening, Anno stepped down on May 7, and founded Team Mirai the next day, May 8 [7].

After Anno left

Because its foundation was Team Anno, many of the engineers and designers moved to Team Mirai. One of the main developers of Kouchou AI also ran for office [7].

The book Broad Listening calls May 2025 a turning point, "a moment when 'deceleration and acceleration happened at the same time'". A small number of members kept holding regular development meetings so that development would not stop. Features that Team Mirai had built on its own copy were also brought back into the original Kouchou AI [7].

Learning from Taiwan

In his commentary on the Japanese edition of PLURALITY, Ken Suzuki writes that Taiwan's practice should not simply be imported as a miraculous success story, and that it is necessary to understand how it really works [12]. So Anno and the DD2030 team visited Taiwan for three days in late January 2025 and interviewed 13 people.

According to Suzuki, what they found was that digital democracy cannot be achieved without an enormous amount of offline effort. He also says that it needs two wheels turning together: citizens' activity and strong involvement by the government [12].

A place to gather

On August 2, 2026, DD2030 held "Digital Democracy Summit 2026", in which Audrey Tang took part. The announced speakers included Anno (Team Mirai), Takae Ito (Democratic Party for the People), Hideto Kawasaki (Liberal Democratic Party, Parliamentary Vice-Minister for Digital Transformation), Manabu Miyasaka (Vice Governor of Tokyo) and Haruyuki Seki [13].

What is debated

  • Isn't it really a Team Mirai organization? DD2030 commits itself to partisan neutrality, and Anno has left the board. The book Broad Listening stresses that Idobata "is not 'owned by Team Mirai'; it is a public asset that anyone can use" [5][11]. However, the same book itself records that the organization grew out of the same base and that many of the people overlapped [7].
  • Who is responsible for open-source citizen participation? One citizen's note.com article criticizes systems like Kouchou AI and Idobata as vague about quality control, accountability and guarantees that the service will be maintained, and argues that it has not been shown that the citizen participation really helps [14].
  • Hard to spread to local governments. DD2030 itself records that after trying the tools, local governments found it hard to move on to full use. It gives as reasons a lack of motivation to use them, a gap in enthusiasm between local government heads and front-line staff, personnel transfers, and a shortage of engineers [7].
  • Reflecting on stopping at listening. Project Coreloop, mentioned above, is the project's own statement of the need to go beyond a democracy that only listens [1]. For criticisms of broad listening itself, see the article on broad listening.

Where it fits in Plurality

The book Plurality does not mention DD2030. But Ken Suzuki, who wrote the commentary for the Japanese edition, is a co-founder, and in that commentary he positions DD2030 as a starting point for rooting digital democracy in Japan [12]. Suzuki's own book なめらかな社会とその敵 (The Nameraka Society and Its Enemies) is introduced in chapter 3-3 of Plurality as an earlier line of thought [12][15]. See the article on the Nameraka Society.

What it works on corresponds to what the book calls augmented deliberation (using technology to deepen discussion). Kouchou AI and Idobata can also be read as efforts to build Japanese versions of Taiwan's vTaiwan and JOIN. DD2030 could be seen as an organization on the building side of the "digital public goods" discussed in chapter 7-0 of the book, but the book does not name DD2030. See also the article on digital public infrastructure.
