What is Idobata?

A Japanese-made discussion tool in which each person talks with an AI in a chat, and AI gathers what was said into shared issues and proposed revisions to policy. Digital Democracy 2030 (DD2030) publishes it for free, and it was used in drawing up Team Mirai's manifesto.

When many people write on a single message board, the loudest voices tend to stand out, and the discussion often fails to come together. Yet listening to people one by one takes far too much effort. "Idobata" is a tool that tries to solve this problem by having an AI listen to each person individually and then connect what it hears.

In short

Idobata is a tool in which each person chats one-on-one with an AI, and AI gathers the content into "everyone's issues" or "proposed revisions to policy" [1][2].

It is built by the Japanese volunteer community Digital Democracy 2030 (DD2030). Its program code is public, so that anyone can reuse it for free (open source) [1].

For example

Team Mirai's "interactive policy platform" (2025 House of Councillors election). The political party Team Mirai launched it on May 16, 2025. When a voter chats with the AI, the AI asks questions and helps the voter put their thoughts into words. At the end, the AI puts together a proposed change to the manifesto (the party's campaign promises) and sends it [2].

Four days later, party leader Takahiro Anno posted that more than 1,300 proposal documents had come in, "more than ten times what we expected". In the end, 8,559 policy proposals were received [2].

A workshop inside Cybozu. The AI asked follow-up questions about what participants had written, digging deeper. The AI read 50 people's chats in a few minutes and produced a report of the themes that came up often and the points where opinions agreed or differed [3].

A residents' meeting in Higashiosaka. After meetings in which residents discussed things with one another, Idobata was used as a place to keep collecting opinions. The AI organized 12 issues, including "From an anxious future to a hopeful one" [4]. → Broad listening in local governments

How it works

Two components

Idobata is made of two components that can be used separately [1].

  • Idobata Vision — A party, local government, company, school or other organizer sets a theme. Users write opinions and questions freely in a chat. The system extracts the important points and organizes them as "key issues". It is the component for widely gathering visions of the future and directions.
  • Idobata Policy — The component for revising an existing draft (such as a manifesto) through dialogue with users. Proposed changes are created as "pull requests" (requests to make a change) on GitHub, a website for sharing programs [1][5].

The README (explanatory document) says that using the two in sequence lets one handle everything from finding problems to making measures concrete, in one continuous flow [1].

Why it was built

The README lists the problems with earlier attempts at discussion: opinions do not come together, only the loud voices remain, and it is hard to connect gathered opinions to policy [1].

At DD2030's launch press conference on January 16, 2025, Anno announced this as the project for "reflecting public opinion in policy". The idea was to build a place for large-scale online discussion, modeled on Taiwan's vTaiwan and JOIN (the Taiwanese government's citizen-participation website) [6][7].

A new form: "connected through AI"

In the book Broad Listening (written by volunteers in the DD2030 community), Hirokazu Nishio's section explains Idobata as a third form of communication [2].

  • The first is a form in which everyone is in the same chat (message boards and the like).
  • The second is a closed form between one person and an AI (ChatGPT and the like).
  • The third is Idobata, in which people "in separate chats" are "connected through AI".

For example, the AIs in Idobata Policy share "the current platform". When a human approves a revision that came out of a dialogue with one person, the AI talking with the next person reads the revised manifesto as it talks [2]. Nishio links this to the argument of the philosopher John Dewey that changes in technology change the shape of "the public" [2]. → Emergent publics

What is debated

  • Not feeling heard. With the interactive policy platform, things ended once a proposal was sent. People could not see how it was handled afterward, and some said they did not feel their opinions had been received. Nishio concludes that a system is needed that lets people follow where their opinion went [2].
  • Proposals too detailed, and clashing. Dozens of revisions piled up on the same passage, and staff had to read them all and rewrite by brute force. Nishio writes that perhaps what the AI should have asked was not so much how to revise the wording as what people are struggling with. Nishio then cites Audrey Tang's procedure for discussion: first gather facts, then feelings, and only after that ask for ideas [2].
  • GitHub turned into a message board as it was. One proposal once drew 236 comments [8]. Barbed exchanges broke out, and some people used Anno's face photo as their icon without permission. On X, misleading information spread that took this for an "internal discussion" within Team Mirai. Nishio writes that GitHub should not have been shown directly, and should have been covered with a dedicated interface [2].
  • Quality of proposals, and responsibility. One citizen's note.com article reports criticisms that the original proposals were of low quality and that the review process should be fixed. It goes on to write that open-source citizen participation leaves responsibility unclear, and even that Team Mirai is effectively throwing away the proposals it gathered. Part of this claim, however, has not been verified [9]. For reference, Team Mirai's policy repository has received 9,686 proposals in total, of which 194 have been merged. Updates to the manifesto on GitHub ended on July 26, 2025 [10][11].
  • Hard to tell what the tool is for. Another note.com writer commented that Idobata Vision is hard for first-time users to understand, and that it feels like a public comment procedure (a government's call for opinions on a draft) [12].
  • Who decides the range of what is heard? An article in the magazine Noema (June 2026) pointed out that the program of the AI chatbot Team Mirai used in the 2026 House of Representatives election had "NG words" (banned words) set for conversations to avoid. (The article does not name that chatbot as Idobata.) Its point is that, together with the constraints on the side of the underlying AI (Claude), the boundaries of political conversation may be set in several places. At the same time, the article credits the tool as democratic in that it did not force consensus and left judgment to the party and voters [13].

Where it fits in Plurality

The book Plurality does not mention Idobata by name. But it is one Japanese implementation of the tools for holding conversations at large scale that chapter 5-4, "Augmented Deliberation", describes (Polis, vTaiwan, and broad listening with AI) [14].

While DD2030's Kouchou AI is a tool for turning gathered voices into a map, Idobata handles the side of listening to voices one by one, pulling them together and connecting them to the next action. The two are two of the three projects announced at DD2030's launch [6].

The book Broad Listening stresses that Idobata "is not 'owned by Team Mirai'; it is a public asset that anyone can use". It says that in 2025 preparations were also under way to introduce it in another party, but these faded away after the person in charge on the other side was reassigned [2].
